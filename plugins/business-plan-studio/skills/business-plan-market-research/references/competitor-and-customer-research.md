## Mapping alternatives

Every customer already solves the problem somehow. Map four kinds: **direct** (same offer, same customer) · **indirect** (different offer, same need) · **substitutes** (doing it in-house, informal sellers, spreadsheets, family) · **doing nothing**. "No competitors" usually means the founder has not looked or the need is weak.

## Finding competitors

Ask the founder whom customers mention. Then search: maps listings in the target area · marketplaces and delivery apps · search engines with the customer's words · social media hashtags and pages · business directories and chambers · trade fair exhibitor lists · app stores · job ads (show who is growing).

| Target customer | who they serve best |
| Channels | how they sell |

| Evidence | links, screenshots noted with dates |

## Comparison grid and positioning

Pick 4–6 buying criteria from customer interviews (price, speed, quality, reliability, location, range, service, language). Score each competitor and the founder 1–5 with a one-line reason. Expect the founder to lose on some criteria. Then choose two axes customers care about and place each player; the open quadrant is a positioning option, but only if customers value it.

## Pricing scan

Collect at least three comparable prices per offer with date, unit, tax treatment and what is included. Compare like with like (same size, service level, delivery). Use the range, not one competitor, to position the price; check it against the founder's cost floor (business-plan-financials or `bizcalc.py`).

## Judging advantage

Ask: what would a well-funded competitor need a year to copy? Durable advantages: lower cost structure, exclusive access (supply, location, licence, partner), know-how and track record, data or network effects, brand and relationships, switching costs. Not advantages on their own: passion, quality, "first mover", features that are easy to copy.

## Customer interviews: principles

- Ask about specific past events ("the last time…"), not opinions about the future ("would you buy…?").
- Do not pitch or describe the solution until the end, if at all.
- Dig into what they did, what it cost in money and time, and what they tried.
- Listen for spending that already happens: money spent on workarounds is the best sign of a real problem.
- Take notes in their words; count patterns afterwards.

## Interview guide (English)

Adapt to B2B (role, company process) or consumers (household, habits).
1. Tell me about your role (or your household) and how [activity] fits in your week.
2. When was the last time you dealt with [problem]? What happened?
3. What did you do about it?
4. What did that cost you, in money and in time?
5. How often does this happen?
6. What have you tried to solve it? What did you like and dislike about each option?
7. How do you decide what to buy for this? Who else is involved?
8. How much do you spend on this today, and from which budget?
9. What would make you switch from what you use now?
10. What would stop you from switching?
11. If this problem disappeared, what would change for you?
12. Who else should I talk to about this?

## Recruiting and sample size

Find interviewees through the founder's network (but not friends and family only), customer lists, LinkedIn, community groups, associations, visiting shops or offices in person, and referrals at the end of each interview. As a rule of thumb, 10–15 interviews with one segment usually show whether a pattern is real; keep going until new interviews stop surprising you. Surveys help to measure how common a pattern is once interviews have found it; avoid "would you buy" questions.

## Synthesis and evidence levels

| Interviewee type | Date | Problem confirmed? | Last occurrence | Cost | Current solution | Spend today | Willing to try or pay? | Quote (real) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|

Summarise with counts ("9 of 12 lost stock last month; average loss about 4000 MAD"). Rate each finding on the evidence ladder: 0 assumption · 1 desk research · 2 interviews · 3 commitment (deposit, letter of intent, pre-order) · 4 paid sales or pilot · 5 repeat purchase. Label findings Verified only with notes or records.
